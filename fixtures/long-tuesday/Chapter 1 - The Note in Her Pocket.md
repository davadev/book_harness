# Chapter 1: The Note in Her Pocket

Friday, the fourteenth of March, 2091, began for Ines Varga with a sound she had not heard in three years: Jonas Reiter not answering his own door.

She knocked twice, the way she always did, two flat knuckle taps and then a pause, and waited in the corridor of the Kestrel Platform's living deck while the wind worked at the steel skin of the building. Forty kilometres of North Sea lay between the platform and the nearest coast, and on mornings like this one the sea made itself felt in every joint of the structure, a long slow groan that rose from the legs and travelled up through the floors into the soles of her feet. She had stopped noticing it years ago. This morning she noticed everything.

"Jonas," she said. "It is ten past six. You said you would bring the coolant logs to breakfast."

Nothing. She tried the handle. The cabin was empty, the bed made with the tight corners he had learned in some earlier life, his boots gone from the rack. On the desk his lamp was still lit, and under the lamp lay a mug of coffee with a skin on it, long cold.

Ines stood very still. Jonas was forty-one years old, an engineer who slept six hours a night and woke at the same minute every day, and he did not leave coffee to go cold. She turned off the lamp, because it seemed the sort of small order a person ought to keep, and went down the stairs to the lower decks.

The Kestrel Platform had been built to harvest the tide. Its legs stood in the water like the legs of a heron, and between them the current turned eight great turbines that fed the grid on the mainland. The living deck, the mess, the laboratories and the six storage holds sat above the waterline in a stack of grey modules. Eleven people lived and worked there, rotating on and off with the supply boat every month, and Ines had been among them for four years, long enough to know every one of them by the way they walked.

She walked now toward Hold Four.

Hold Four was the biggest of the holds and the coldest, and for the past year it had been hers and Jonas's. Director Pike had given them the space, and a budget that he described, with the dry humour of a man who had spent his life writing budgets, as "generous in the sense that nobody will ever audit it." Inside, under a ceiling of exposed pipes, stood the machine they called the Fold. It was a ring of cadmium glass three metres across, mounted on a steel cradle, wired to the platform's main bus through a cable as thick as Ines's thigh. When the turbines gave it forty megawatts it folded a small region of space back across itself and, if it was asked correctly, let a person step through the fold and come out at an earlier time. It could not send anything further back than seventy-two hours. Nobody knew why. Ines had spent two years trying to understand the limit and had arrived at a number, a theory, and a very good reason not to publish either.

The door of Hold Four was unlocked.

That alone was wrong. Jonas locked the hold every night; it was the single rule of his that Pike had never been able to bend. Ines pushed the door open with two fingers and the cold came out to meet her, and with it the smell of hot metal, which should not have been there at all.

Jonas lay on the floor in front of the cradle, on his back, one arm flung out. His eyes were open. The glass ring behind him was dark and cracked from edge to edge, and a long scorch ran across the deck from the cradle to his body like the track of a lightning strike.

She did not scream. She was surprised by that, later, when she thought about it. She crossed the floor and knelt, and put two fingers to his throat as she had been taught in the platform's first-aid course, and there was nothing. His skin was cool but not cold. He wore the grey work jacket with the torn cuff. His left hand lay open on the steel, palm up, and she saw the old scar there, the pale shiny pucker the coolant had left when it burned him in the accident of 2088, three years before the Fold had ever been switched on. She had held that hand while the medic cleaned it. She knew the shape of the scar the way she knew her own signature.

"Oh, Jonas," Ines said. "What did you do?"

The platform groaned. Somewhere above her a pump cycled on.

She stayed where she was for perhaps a minute. Then, because there was nothing else to do and because she had always been a person who responded to disaster by counting things, she began to count. The ring was cracked, which meant the Fold had been fired at full power and had failed. The scorch ran from the cradle, which meant the discharge had been direct. The bus cable was still hot where it entered the cradle, which meant it had happened within the last few hours. The clock on the wall above the door said six-twenty. The log terminal on the bench showed a last entry at eleven minutes past one in the morning: *Run 41. Power 40. Aborted.* She had seen Run 40 two days earlier. Run 41 was Jonas's, and she had not known about it.

She checked the log twice, in case she had misread the number. Then she saw the second thing that was wrong with the room.

Jonas's coffee mug in the cabin had been cold, but there was a second mug on the bench by the terminal, and it was warm.

Ines put her hand around it. It was her own mug, the green one with the chipped handle that she kept in the mess. Somebody had carried it down from the mess and filled it with coffee, and that somebody had stood here, in this room, within the last half hour.

"Hello?" she said.

The hold was a big space full of pipes and shadow and the dead glass ring. No one answered. She turned in a slow circle. The door she had come through stood open behind her. The other door, the freight door at the far end that led to the lift, was shut and bolted from the inside.

She put her hand in the pocket of her jacket for her radio, and her fingers closed on paper.

It was a single sheet, folded in four, and she had not put it there. She unfolded it in the cold light of the hold and read it, and for the second time that morning she did not scream.

The handwriting was her own. There was no mistaking it: the way she crossed her sevens, the way her lowercase *g* never quite closed. The ink was blue, from the pen she kept in the left pocket of her jacket. The note was short.

*Ines. Jonas is dead and it is not your fault, but it will be unless you do exactly what this says. Go back three days. Arrive in Hold Four on Tuesday the eleventh at ten in the morning. Do not tell Pike. Do not touch the log. Do not let Jonas run the Fold on Thursday night. I am sorry about the coffee. I needed you to find the room still warm. Trust the handwriting. — I.*

She read it three times. Then she folded it and put it in her inner pocket, against her ribs, and sat down on an upturned crate because her legs had stopped being reliable.

There were, she knew, three ways to read a note like that. The first was that it was a hoax. Pike was capable of many things, but not of forging her handwriting. The second was that she had written it herself and forgotten, which was the explanation a sensible psychiatrist would offer and which fell apart the moment she tested it against the ink. The third was the explanation that she had spent two years building a machine to make possible.

She stood and walked to the bench. The terminal showed the Fold's state: ring cracked, cradle intact, bus live. She opened the maintenance panel at the base of the cradle and found what she had half expected, which was that the control module had survived the discharge. It was a small thing, a grey block the size of a paperback, and it kept its own record of every run in a memory that did not depend on the platform's log. She plugged in her slate and read it.

Run 41 had begun at 1:07 on Friday morning. Jonas had set the power at forty megawatts and the destination at *minus seventy-two hours*, the longest jump the Fold could make. He had not been trying to go back to Tuesday the eleventh. He had been aiming further back than that.

There was a second entry, below the first, which the platform's own log had not recorded. Run 42 had begun at 1:09 and ended at 1:09. Its destination field was empty. Its power field read *40*. Its duration was zero seconds. Someone had fired the Fold, and it had not folded anything.

"Or it did," Ines said aloud, "and it did not go anywhere I can read."

She sat back on her heels. The temperature in the hold was dropping toward the platform's winter standard of nine degrees, and she shivered. A zero-second run was the Fold's way of recording a jump that had left no mark on its own memory, which in two years of testing had meant exactly one thing: a person had gone through, and had come out somewhere that was not on this branch of the log.

Somebody had left Hold Four at nine minutes past one that morning. Somebody had stood here afterwards and poured a cup of coffee into Ines's green mug. And Jonas lay dead on the floor, killed by a discharge from a machine he had built.

She was not a brave woman. She had always been precise, which people confused with bravery, and she was precise now. She wrote on her slate, in the notes she kept for the research file: *Friday 14 March, 06:31. Found J.R. deceased, Hold Four. Fold fired at 01:07 and 01:09. Note in my handwriting in my pocket. I am going to follow it.* Then she crossed out the last sentence, because a person who wrote *I am going to follow it* in the research file was a person who expected to be read by someone later, and she did not want to be read yet.

She was still sitting on the floor when the door opened behind her and Director Halloran Pike came in with a mug of tea in one hand.

He was a heavy, stooped man of sixty-two, with a white beard clipped close and a habit of standing as though the floor were tilting beneath him. He took in the scene in a single sweep of his tired grey eyes: the cracked ring, the scorch, the body. He set his tea carefully on the nearest crate.

"How long has he been here?" Pike said.

"I found him at twenty past six. The log says the last run was at one in the morning."

"Did you touch anything?"

"His throat. And the control module."

"Don't touch anything else." He knelt, heavily, and looked at Jonas for a long moment. His face did something complicated and then went still. "I told him," he said, very quietly. "I told him on Wednesday, Ines. I said, no more full power until the ring has been inspected. He said he understood."

"He never understood anything he did not want to."

"No." Pike straightened, one hand on his knee. "I'll have to call the mainland. A fatality on a research platform. There will be an inquiry, and the Fold will be sealed, and everything we have done here will be in a file in an office in the capital within a week." He looked at her. "You know what that means."

She knew. It meant the end of the project. It meant the end of Jonas's life's work, and of two years of her own, and of the only theory she had ever believed in. She held the note against her ribs through her jacket and said nothing.

"Who else has been down here this morning?" Pike asked.

"I don't know. I came in alone."

"The freight door is bolted inside."

"I saw."

"So whoever poured that coffee," Pike said slowly, "came in by the main door and left by the main door. Between half past six and a quarter past, if the mug is still warm." He frowned at the bench. "Or whoever it was never left at all."

He looked around the hold, and so did she, and for the first time Ines noticed how many shadows the space had. Crates, pipes, the dark flank of the cradle. Nothing moved.

"I'll call the mainland at seven," Pike said. "Until then I want the hold locked and a person on the door. Dev Anand can sit there, he is awake and he has nothing better to do. Go and eat something, Ines. You are the colour of paper."

She went. At the door she looked back once, and saw Pike standing over Jonas with his big hands hanging open at his sides, a man who had just watched the thing he had spent his money on kill the person who built it.

She did not go to the mess. She went up to her cabin and sat on the bed with her slate in her lap, and read through everything she knew about the Fold, from the first sketch on a napkin in 2089 to the run on Monday the third of March, eleven days ago, when the ring had first carried a sealed weather balloon back six hours and returned it with a perfectly synchronised clock. She had been in the room. She and Jonas had danced, actually danced, in the cold of Hold Four, two serious people with a very small success between them.

That was the Fold's whole history: one balloon, and then a month of careful runs, each longer than the last, until Jonas had put his own hand through the fold on the Tuesday and brought it back with the cuff of his jacket torn. She had screamed at him for that. He had laughed.

She read her own note again, and noticed a detail she had missed in the hold. The note said *Arrive in Hold Four on Tuesday the eleventh at ten in the morning.* Ten in the morning was a specific hour. It was the hour at which, according to the log, nobody would be in the hold, because Pike's Tuesday inspection of the turbines always ran from nine until noon. Whoever had written the note knew the platform's routine. That was not remarkable if she had written it herself.

What was remarkable was the last line. *I am sorry about the coffee. I needed you to find the room still warm.*

If the note-writer had been in Hold Four at about six that morning, she had been waiting for Ines to come. She had been standing in the shadow of the cradle, or behind the freight door, and she had slipped into Ines's pocket the one piece of paper that could change everything. And then she had gone, not out of the main door where Pike might meet her, but through the only exit that left no mark on any log.

Ines closed her eyes. She did the arithmetic she had avoided all morning.

The Fold's limit was seventy-two hours. Tuesday at ten was seventy-two hours before Friday at ten. It was exactly as far back as the machine could send her, and the note had been careful to say so. Whoever had written it had known where the limit lay. And whoever had written it had come from somewhere after this Friday, because a person could not carry a note from the future into the past unless she had first lived through the future.

"Jonas dies on Friday," Ines said to the empty cabin. "Whoever she is, she has already seen what happens after that."

She stood and went to the window. Outside, the sea was the colour of dishwater, and far to the east a grey line of weather was moving across it. The supply boat was due on Monday. On the horizon a single gull was working against the wind, going nowhere.

It was a quarter to eight. The platform had its morning noise: the clatter of the mess, someone laughing too loudly in the corridor, the thump of a door. Dev Anand was already sitting outside Hold Four with his back to the wall, a young technician with a shaved head and a soft voice, and when she came down at eight and said she needed a minute inside he hesitated for the length of a breath and then let her in. He did not ask what for. Nobody on the platform asked Ines Varga what for, any more.

Hold Four was colder than before. They had covered Jonas with a grey blanket, and the ring watched from behind him like a single dead eye.

"I'm sorry," she said to the blanket. "I'll be back before you know it."

She worked quickly. The cradle's power relays had survived, and Run 43 would not draw more than a third of the platform's capacity if she kept it to six kilograms of mass, a single person, a slate, and a pen. She disarmed the safety interlock, the one Jonas had insisted on and she had never liked, and set the destination to *minus seventy-two hours, minus zero minutes*. She did not set the power to forty. She set it to thirty-eight, because the ring was cracked and she did not trust it to carry forty twice.

She wrote one more line in the research file, a line she was sure the future reader would find: *Fri 14 March, 08:14. Following the note.* Then she saved the file, put the slate in her left pocket, and stepped through the broken glass ring into the cold.

For a moment there was no hold, and no Friday, and no Ines. There was a high clean note, like a wet finger on the rim of a glass, and under it the long groan of the platform's legs, which went on and on and then stopped.

She opened her eyes. The ring was whole and bright behind her. The lights in the hold were on. The scorch on the floor had gone, and so had the blanket, and so had the body.

On the wall above the door, the clock said ten past ten. Under it somebody had pinned a calendar, one of the free ones from the mainland supplier, with a red ring drawn around the eleventh of March.

Voices were coming from the corridor. One of them was Jonas's.
