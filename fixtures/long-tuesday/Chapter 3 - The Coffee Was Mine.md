# Chapter 3: The Coffee Was Mine

The machine shop key opened nothing she needed, and Ines Varga stood in the dark outer stairwell turning it over in her fingers like a coin she had been cheated with.

He had given her the wrong key on purpose. That was clear. Jonas Reiter had pressed it into her hand in the crush after Pike's announcement, had looked her in the face, and had let her believe it was the key to Hold Four. He had always been a better liar than she was. He had not been lying about the promise to sleep in the mess; he had meant that, until the moment he heard the word *closure*, and then he had decided that a promise made to one person in one room could not bind a man whose whole life was being closed.

She went down the outer stairs into the wind. It was a few minutes before midnight on Thursday the thirteenth of March, and the platform had settled into its night noises, the pumps cycling, the deep groan of the legs. The sea was loud. She was thirty-six hours short of sleep and her cheek throbbed where the glass had caught it.

At the foot of the stairs, a sliver of light lay under the door of Hold Four.

There was a second way in, the freight door at the far end, which opened onto the lift shaft. Ines took the lift, up one floor and down another, through the dark guts of the platform. She did not turn on the light. When the lift stopped she slid the bolt that Jonas had fitted himself and stepped through, and found herself behind the cradle with the great ring rising above her like the frame of a doorway to nowhere.

He was at the terminal. He had taken off his jacket and hung it over a crate, and in shirtsleeves in the cold with his head bent over the screen he looked like what he was: a man doing the work he loved on the last night he would be allowed to do it. His right hand moved over the keys, and on its palm, when he turned it to reach the switch, the old coolant scar showed pale and shiny.

"You came by the lift," Jonas said, without looking up. "I heard the bolt. You should oil it."

"You gave me the wrong key."

"Yes."

"You promised."

"I did." He tapped one last key, and the ring woke, a faint glassy shimmer along its inner edge like frost climbing a window. "Ines, I'm sorry. I did mean it, at the time. I want you to know that I meant it."

"Turn it off."

"I can't." He swung round on the stool. He looked calm, which frightened her more than anything. "Listen to me for two minutes. I'll make it quick. Then you can decide whether to hit me."

She stood where she was, with her hand on the cold steel of the cradle.

"The hairline in the ring," Jonas said. "I told you it wasn't there on Monday. I measured it myself with the gauge, at three in the afternoon, and it was clean to a tenth of a millimetre. Pike saw it on Tuesday night. In between there are thirty hours, and in those thirty hours somebody or something put a flaw in a ring of cadmium glass that does not crack. I have checked every record I can. Nobody entered this hold between Monday evening and Tuesday morning except myself, and then you, from the other end, at ten past ten."

"Me."

"You." He spread his hands. "You came through at thirty-eight megawatts on a ring that had already been cracked once, by the discharge on Friday. The ring that sent you was the ring that broke. And it carried the crack with it. I mean that it is the *same* crack, Ines. It was not made on Tuesday at all. It is the Friday crack arriving three days early, because you did."

For a moment the hold was very still.

She had thought of it, on the bed in cabin thirty-one, in the dark. She had thought of it and put it away. The Fold had a limit of seventy-two hours, and the limit was a thing she had measured, and tested, and written down. *Seventy-two hours. No further.* And the ring's flaw had appeared exactly seventy-two hours after Friday's discharge, which could not be a coincidence, because there are no coincidences in a theory that works.

"I made it," Ines said. "The crack. By coming back."

"We both made it. I am the one who set the Fold for you. You are the one who took the step." His voice shook, a very little. "And it will happen again, because it already has. The ring is cracked now. If you or I or anyone jumps from this platform tonight, the discharge will reopen it, and the next jump will be a day shorter. The ring will go on tearing, and the time you can reach will shrink, and the Fold will end as a dark piece of glass in a closed room. That is what your Friday was. That is the thing I saw coming when Pike said *regrettably*."

"So you decided to go back further."

"I decided to go to the beginning." He turned to the screen and she saw the number there. *Minus seventy-two hours. Power forty.* "It is the Friday jump that damaged the ring. If I leave before Friday, before the first crack, and arrive on Tuesday at one in the morning, I can be standing in front of the cradle when you step out of it on Tuesday morning. I can talk you out of it. There will never be a crack. The Fold is sound and Pike has no reason to close it."

"And you?"

"I'm the one person who doesn't need to be in the future," Jonas said, and smiled. "I will have been dead there. It is the only way to be sure."

She heard the sentence a second time, and understood it. Then she crossed the floor in four steps and put her hand on the breaker, the big red lever on the wall that cut the Fold from the main bus.

"Ines. Don't."

"You are not going to die for a theory."

"It isn't a theory. It is arithmetic." He had stood up and was walking toward her, slowly, with his scarred hand held out like a man approaching a nervous horse. "You have seen the log. Run 41, power 40, aborted. Do you not understand what that means? It means that I tried and *failed*. There is a Friday in which I did this and was killed by it. I do not have a choice about that Friday. All I can do is try to be a better version of myself in the one before."

"There is no before. There is no—" She stopped. She felt something go through her, cold and complete, like a pane of glass coming down. "You said *I*. You said you tried and failed. You have not tried yet."

"No," Jonas said. "But I will."

"Then the log is wrong. The log says it happens and you want it to be true. You are *creating* the thing that kills you." She was crying and had not noticed. "Jonas. Listen. If you do nothing tonight, the ring stays cracked, Pike closes the platform, and you live. You live. It is the only branch where you live."

"And the other you? The one who goes back on Friday, with a note in her pocket?" His eyes were very kind. "She is the one who needs me to go. She exists because I fire the Fold. If I do not, your Friday has no note, and no coffee, and no you."

She stood with her hand on the breaker, and understood that he was right, and that the rightness had no use.

The ring hummed. It had been charging while they spoke, and the frost along its inner edge had spread to a white halo. The clock on the wall said four minutes to one. A pump overhead cycled on, and a whole stack of loose pipes shivered together along the ceiling.

"Let me come with you," she said.

"The ring will not carry two. You know it won't. At forty megawatts with a flaw in the glass it will barely carry one." He took her hand off the breaker, gently, one finger at a time. "And you are the one person on this platform I cannot lose twice."

He kissed her once on the forehead. It was the first time. It was dry and quick and smelled of cold metal and machine oil.

She did not stop him. She would remember that, afterwards, for the rest of her life: that she stood there with her wet face and her hand still warm from his, and let him go to the cradle and set his feet on the steel.

He was standing in the ring at seven minutes past one when the log terminal beeped and the lights went out.

---

She did not remember the discharge. She remembered the instant before it, in which she saw Jonas's face in the white glare, calm and astonished like the face of a man who has heard a very good joke, and she remembered a sound like a sheet of iron being torn in half. After that there was nothing for some time.

When she opened her eyes she was lying on the floor with her cheek against cold steel and a taste of copper in her mouth. The lights were on. The ring behind the cradle was black and starred from edge to edge, and a long scorched line ran from its foot across the deck to where Jonas lay.

It was five past one by the clock. She lay and watched the second hand go round, and when she could stand she crawled to him and put two fingers to his throat. The skin was cool. There was nothing under it.

His left hand lay open. The scar on the palm shone in the light. She took it in both of hers and held it for what felt like a very long time, until her own hands were numb.

"You fool," Ines said. "You wonderful, stupid, brave fool."

At nine minutes past one she did the other thing she remembered doing. She got to her feet, shaking, and went to the terminal and set the destination to *minus seventy-two hours*. She did not know why. She told herself that if the Fold had carried him, however briefly, it might carry her, that he might be waiting for her on a different Tuesday, that the glass might hold for one more breath. She put her hand on the control and fired it.

The ring flared, screamed, and went out. Nothing moved. The record on the module read *Run 42. Power 40. Duration 0.* She was standing exactly where she had been.

It did not work because there was nothing left that could work. She understood that, standing there. The Fold had one flawed ring and a limit of ninety-six hours, a number she had once written on a napkin and never believed, and it had burned its last good jump on Jonas.

She sat down on the floor beside him and stayed there for a long time.

---

There is a kind of grief that arrives all at once and a kind that arrives in stages, like weather. Hers began as the first kind and became the second at about three in the morning, when she found that she had stopped weeping and was sitting cross-legged in the cold with her slate in her lap, writing.

She did not quite know she was doing it. She knew only that her hand was moving, and that the sentences in front of her were coming out in an order she recognised. *Ines. Jonas is dead and it is not your fault, but it will be unless you do exactly what this says.* She stopped. She read the line again. She had read it before. She had read it on Friday morning, in this room, with her fingers numb and the cold at her ankles, and she had thought it was a note from someone who had already lived through everything.

It was not. It was a note from someone who was living through it now, and had only remembered what the note said.

"Oh," she said aloud. "Oh, you have to be joking."

The thing had no beginning. That was the horror and the beauty of it. She had gone back because a note told her to, and the note existed because she had gone back and had written it, from memory, out of a sentence she had read that was only there because she had written it. No one had ever composed it. It had simply always been true. She laughed, a thin shaky sound in the empty hold, and then stopped laughing because it had begun to sound like crying.

She finished the note anyway. *Go back three days. Arrive in Hold Four on Tuesday the eleventh at ten in the morning.* She did not alter a word. She knew, with the odd clarity of someone who has been handed a script, that altering a single word would do something she did not wish to find out. She wrote it in blue ink with the pen she kept in her left pocket, and she crossed her sevens and left her *g* open.

At the end she wrote *I am sorry about the coffee*, and it took her a while to find out why.

The coffee. She went to the mess, up the stairs in her stockinged feet, and filled her green mug at the urn, and carried it back down, and set it on the bench. She had no idea whether the Ines who would find her mug was one of the lucky ones, a woman who could read it as a sign. All she knew was that the note said *I needed you to find the room still warm*, and the only thing that would keep the room warm was a hot mug at half past six, and the only person on the platform who could make it so was herself. She was setting a table for a guest who had already arrived.

At twenty past five she emptied the cold coffee and made a fresh one. At six she made another.

She hid behind the cradle, where she had hidden on Tuesday, and she waited. The platform groaned. Pumps came on and went off. At six minutes past six somebody walked along the corridor outside, a heavy tread, a man in rubber boots going out to the turbine bay, and she held her breath until he had gone by.

At ten past six, a knock sounded on a door somewhere far above: two flat taps, a pause.

She knew that knock. She had been giving it for four years. It was her own.

She stood with her back against the cradle, and her heart going like a pump, and listened to herself come down the stairs.

---

It was the strangest five minutes of her life. She heard the door of the hold open, the light step of her own feet on the deck, the small sharp breath of a woman who has found a thing she does not understand. She heard her own voice say, "Oh, Jonas. What did you do?" It was lower than she had thought. It was a good deal sadder.

She waited for the Ines on the floor to kneel. She waited for the fingers on the throat. And when the woman at the cradle bent forward with her back to the shadows and her jacket pocket wide open on the right side, the other one slipped out from behind the steel with a sheet of folded paper in her hand.

It was nothing. It was a thing she did every day, to anyone: a hand passing a hand. She slid the note into the pocket, felt it settle against a lining she knew, and withdrew. She had never in her life been so quiet.

Nobody saw her go. She slipped into the lift and pulled the freight door shut behind her and went up through the dark of the platform, and when the cage lifted her into the cold stairwell above, she had to put her forehead against the steel wall until the world stopped moving.

At ten to seven Pike came down to Hold Four with his mug of tea. At eight she saw Dev Anand take his seat outside the door. At fourteen minutes past eight she watched from the gantry above the hold's skylight as a woman with a cut on her cheek walked into the ring.

She did not look away.

The woman was thirty-eight years old. She was frightened and very brave. She wore a grey jacket with a bruise-coloured stain on one cuff, and she stood for a moment in the middle of the glass with her hands open at her sides, as people do who are not sure whether they are about to be struck. Then the ring flared white, and she was gone.

There was no thunder. There was only a faint high note, a wet finger on a glass, and the groan of the platform's legs, which went on and on and then stopped.

Ines stood on the gantry for a long while.

She was, she thought, the only Ines Varga on the Kestrel Platform now, a woman of thirty-six who had lived three days twice and found no way to be ten years older than she was. She was alone in the cold, in a grey jacket, with a cut on her cheek. She had a green mug of coffee gone cold in a hold below, a dead man's key in her pocket, and a note in her own hand that she could never read again, because it had gone where it was always meant to go.

On the ceiling pipe of Hold Four, far below her, a dark blue cap was still stuck where it had landed on the third of March, three weeks ago to the day. Nobody had thought to bring a pole.

She was going to have to tell Pike something. She did not yet know what. She would have to say that she had found Jonas, and tell the truth about the ring, and decide whether the Fold would be buried with him or left to be found by some other person on some other morning. There would be an inquiry, and the mainland, and a hundred small lies, and one very large and quiet one.

She would sit in the mess and drink her tea. The sea would groan. At nine, Dev Anand would come to say Pike wanted her; and she would say yes, she was coming.

Somewhere down in the dark, the glass ring ticked once, cooling, like a clock that has been wound for the last time.

Ines Varga took the key out of her pocket, the right key this time, the one that locked Hold Four for good, and went down the stairs to be asked.
