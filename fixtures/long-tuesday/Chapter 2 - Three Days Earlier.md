# Chapter 2: Three Days Earlier

The first rule of meeting yourself, Ines Varga decided, was not to be in the room.

She stood behind the cradle of the Fold with her back against cold steel and listened to Jonas's voice come down the corridor, cheerful and unhurried and very much alive. The sound of it went through her like a blade. She had knelt beside his body not two hours ago, by her own clock. She pressed the heel of her hand against her mouth and stayed there until the voice had gone past the door and out of hearing, a man on the telephone telling somebody on the mainland that the ring would be ready by Thursday.

It was Tuesday the eleventh of March, 2091, ten minutes past ten in the morning. The calendar said so, and so did the log terminal on the bench, and so did her own slate when she took it out with shaking fingers and saw the date on its face rewrite itself to match. The ring behind her was whole. The scorch mark was gone. Hold Four had the stale, sweetish smell of a room where a lot of careful work was done, and nothing in it was broken.

She noticed her own breathing, and slowed it. Then she began, in the usual way, to count.

There were nine things in the hold she could name that had been different on Friday morning. The ring, the floor, the bench, Jonas's mug of tea on the terminal, the warm lamp, the log, the cable, the calendar with its red circle, and the quiet. The quiet was the biggest difference. On Friday the cold had come to meet her like an accusation. Now the room was merely cool, and busy, and unfinished, the way a workshop is on a day when the people who built it are about to walk back in.

She was thirty-eight years old and she had a cut on her left cheekbone where the ring's glass had flicked her during the discharge, and her hands would not stop moving. She put them in her pockets. The paper was still there, against her ribs: the note, in her own hand. She took it out and read it once more, as one touches a bruise.

*Do not tell Pike. Do not touch the log. Do not let Jonas run the Fold on Thursday night.*

Thursday night. That was in two days and thirteen hours. In a sensible universe it was an enormous quantity of time.

"Right," Ines said, very softly, to herself, and stepped out from behind the cradle.

She had four problems, and she listed them in order, as she did everything. The first was that there was already an Ines Varga on the platform, three days behind her in experience, living through Tuesday for the first time, who would be in the turbine bay with Pike until noon and then in the lab. She must not meet that Ines. She did not know what would happen if the two of them stood in the same room, and she was not willing to learn by experiment. The second problem was that she needed somewhere to sleep that was not her own cabin. The third was that she needed to talk to Jonas. And the fourth was the one she did not look at directly: that she did not know why Jonas had gone down to the hold on Thursday night and fired the Fold at its full limit, because on Tuesday she, the other she, had not known either.

She went first to the stores. In the storage hold next to Hold Four, behind the spare insulation, there was a camp bed and a sleeping bag left over from the commissioning crew. She dragged them into the corner behind a stack of crates and arranged them out of the line of sight of the door. Then she sat down on the bed and tried to think like a person who was about to commit a crime against her own history.

Nobody must know. That was what the note said, and the note was right. There were nine of them on the platform that week, with the other two away on the mainland until the boat on Monday, and nine people could not keep a secret of this size for a day. Pike would end the project. Dev Anand, who adored the Fold the way a child adores a lighthouse, would try to use it. Mirela Costa, the medic, would sedate her and radio for a helicopter. And Jonas.

Jonas she would have to tell. There was no other way. She would simply have to find the words.

---

She found him at four in the afternoon, in the machine shop on the second deck, bent over a lathe with his sleeves rolled up. He had always worked with his sleeves rolled up, and in the strip light the scar on his left palm shone like a coin as he turned the workpiece over. He was forty-one years old. He had a long, tired, clever face, and a way of listening with his whole head tilted that made people tell him things.

He looked up and saw her and went perfectly still.

"Ines," he said.

"Don't call out. Don't call anybody. Is the door shut?"

"It is." Very slowly, he put down the file in his hand. He looked at the cut on her cheek, and at the grey in the shadows under her eyes, and at the clothes she had been wearing for thirty hours. "You were at lunch with Pike. I saw you at one. You wore the blue jersey." He swallowed. "You are not wearing the blue jersey."

"No."

"There are two of you."

"There are two of me," Ines said. "She is in the lab right now with her feet on a stool, thinking about coolant. I'm the one who came back."

It was the sort of silence in which a man could be heard thinking. A pump somewhere in the platform clanked and went quiet. Jonas Reiter pulled out a stool from under the bench and sat down on it, carefully, like a man lowering a very full glass.

"How far?" he said.

"Three days. Friday morning, ten past eight. Seventy-two hours back to Tuesday at ten past ten." She heard her own voice, level and absurd. "I cannot go back further. You know that. The limit held."

"It held." He rubbed his face with both hands. "That is a thing I wanted to be wrong about. For a year I wanted it to be wrong." Then he lowered his hands and looked at her, and she saw him stop being afraid and start being interested, which was the way Jonas had always dealt with fear. "Prove it."

"How?"

"Tell me something she wouldn't know yet."

"Thursday night," Ines said. She had decided to begin with the worst. "You go down to Hold Four at one in the morning on Friday, and you fire the Fold at forty megawatts with a destination of minus seventy-two hours. The ring cracks and you are killed."

He did not flinch. He sat with his head tilted and his fingers laced and she saw the information go into him, and be weighed, and be put somewhere.

"That is not proof," he said gently. "That is a threat."

"Then here is a proof. On the third of March, eight days ago, when we sent the balloon back six hours, you took your cap off and threw it at the ceiling. It stuck on the pipe. It is still there. And afterwards in the mess you told me you had never been so happy as when the balloon's clock agreed with the platform's. You said it was like hearing the sea go quiet." She tried to smile. "I haven't told anyone that. Nor has she. Nor have you."

His mouth worked.

"My cap is still on the pipe," Jonas said, in an odd, small voice. "I've been meaning to get a pole."

"I know."

"All right." He took a very deep breath. He looked, for the first time, like a man who had stopped standing on one side of a door and stepped over its threshold. "All right. Tell me the rest."

She told him. It took forty minutes. She told him about the cold of the hold, and the cradle, and the long scorch on the floor; about the log that said *Run 41. Power 40. Aborted.* About the warm green mug, and the note in her pocket. She told him that the handwriting had been her own. She did not tell him that she had held his scarred hand and felt that it was cool. She did not think he needed that.

When she finished, he got up and walked to the window. The machine shop looked out over the south legs of the platform, and the eight great turbines turning under the swell. A cormorant sat on a rail below, drying its wings.

"I don't understand one thing," he said. "I would never run the Fold at full power against Pike's order. I'd never do it with a cracked ring, and the ring is not cracked now."

"Isn't it?"

"It was inspected on Monday. Whole." He frowned at the glass. "What would make me do that?"

"I don't know," Ines said. "That's what I came back to find out."

He nodded slowly. He was still looking at the cormorant. "Pike told me something on Wednesday," he said, "in your timeline, I mean. I mean, he will tell me. He told you he had told me, you said. 'No more full power until the ring has been inspected.'"

"On Wednesday. Yes."

"But the ring *will* have been inspected. I inspected it myself on Monday." He turned. "Why would Pike say that, if the ring is sound?"

"Because he doesn't think it is."

"Because he has been told it isn't," Jonas said. His face had changed. "By somebody."

She had not thought of that. She stared at him, and the arithmetic she had done in the cabin on Friday shifted a little, like a floor under a heavy load. If someone had told Pike the ring was flawed, then someone had a reason to want the Fold sealed, or wanted Jonas to go and prove it was not.

"Who else knows the Fold works?" Ines said.

"Besides us? Pike. He has seen the balloon." Jonas counted on his fingers. "Dev, because he helped me carry the cradle. Mirela, because she signed the form for my hand when I put it through. That is all. Eleven people on the platform in a normal week and four of them know. Nine, this week, with Okafor and Lindqvist away." He glanced up. "The others think we are building a very expensive tidal sensor."

"Four, then. Pike, Dev, Mirela and you."

"And you. And now you."

She sat down on a stool and put her head in her hands. It occurred to her that it was a ridiculous thing to have crossed seventy-two hours and found oneself so tired.

"You need to sleep," Jonas said. "And eat. And you need to not walk about the platform wearing your own face." He rubbed his chin. "There's a cabin on the third deck nobody uses. I'll give you the key."

"I have a camp bed in the storage hold."

"That is a terrible place." He fished in his pocket and held out a key on a loop of green cord. "Third deck, number thirty-one. It has a heater. Don't tell me you have no right to take it."

She took the key. For a moment her fingers closed over his, and she felt the shape of the scar under her thumb, the same pale shiny pucker, the same small continent of damaged skin. She thought: *I will not let this hand be cool.*

"Jonas," she said. "Promise me. Not Thursday. Not at full power. Not alone."

"I promise," Jonas Reiter said, and held her gaze, and did not look away.

---

She slept for fourteen hours in cabin thirty-one on the third deck, and woke in the dark with the heater ticking and a gull screaming somewhere. It was Wednesday morning. She had dreamed of nothing at all.

She spent the day behind a locked door with her slate, trying to work out what she knew. The facts made a very small list. The Fold had been fired twice after midnight on Friday, at 01:07 and 01:09, and the second run had left a zero-length record. Jonas had died in the first of them. Somebody had poured coffee into her mug. Somebody had told Pike the ring was unsafe.

At two in the afternoon, she risked going out. She wore a navy coat belonging to Dev Anand, borrowed from the peg outside the lab, with the hood up, and she kept to the outer stairs where no one ever walked. On the second landing she found herself looking through a window into the mess, and saw herself there.

The other Ines, the one in the blue jersey, sat at the long table with a bowl of soup, reading a printout and tapping the table with a pen. She looked tired. She looked, Ines thought with a pang of tenderness she hadn't expected, rather lovely, in the thoughtless way of people who have not yet been frightened. Her hair was pulled back. She did not have the cut on her cheekbone. She did not know that Jonas would die.

Ines watched for exactly one minute. Then Dev Anand came in with a tray, sat opposite the other Ines, and began to talk, and she backed away from the window and went on down the stairs with her hood drawn forward and her heart beating like a fist on a door.

She found Pike at the foot of the lower stair. He was alone, with his tea, looking out at the sea.

"Director," she said, from under the hood.

He turned, startled, and she realised too late that she had spoken in her own voice. His grey eyes narrowed. For one terrible second she thought he knew.

"Ines," he said. "I thought you were in the mess. Was that Dev's coat?" He frowned. "You have a cut."

"I walked into a door."

"On a platform with doors that open both ways." But he looked away, politely, the way a tired man declines an argument. "Have you spoken with Jonas about the ring?"

"What about the ring?"

"I inspected it myself last night. There is a hairline in the glass at the lower edge. Very fine. I would not trust it past thirty." He rubbed the back of his neck. "He insists it is sound. I have told him no more full power until we have had it looked at."

"Who told you the ring was flawed?"

Pike's eyebrows rose. "Nobody told me. I looked."

"Nobody mentioned it first? Mirela, or Dev?"

"Why would anybody mention it first?" He studied her. "Ines, you are not well. Go and lie down."

She went, with a sick certainty that she had made a mistake, and then, an hour later, a second certainty that she had not. A hairline flaw in a ring of cadmium glass was no more than the Fold's own usual stress, a thing Jonas could weigh and measure. It was a real thing and Pike had really seen it. Nobody had needed to tell him.

She lay on the bed in cabin thirty-one and stared at the ceiling. The limit was seventy-two hours; Jonas had never in his life disobeyed an order he understood; and a hairline flaw would have been reason enough for him to say *no more full power*, himself. Yet on Thursday night he had gone down to Hold Four.

She tried the question from another side. Why would a man fire the Fold at forty megawatts, to the longest jump it could make?

Because he wanted to go back as far as he could.

She sat up.

Because there was something he needed to undo. Because on Wednesday, or on Thursday, something would happen that would make Jonas Reiter, who never in his life ran a test he had not planned for a month, climb down to Hold Four in the middle of the night and throw himself at the oldest thing the machine could reach. What would that be?

She put her hand to the cut on her cheek and felt it throb.

"Me," she whispered. "I would be what he wants to undo. The day I tell him he dies."

---

On Thursday, the thirteenth, she went to see him in the morning and found him at the bench in Hold Four with the ring lowered on its cradle and a lamp clipped to the frame. He was running a thumbnail along the lower edge of the glass.

"Don't," he said, without turning. "I know. I have found it."

"You said it was sound."

"I said it was inspected. It is cracked. A hairline, like the Director said. It was not there on Monday." He straightened, rubbing his lower back with the heel of his hand. "Nothing in a ring that size makes a hairline in three days by itself."

"Somebody did it."

"Or something did." He met her eyes. "Ines, I have been thinking about what you told me. If a person from Friday has already been here, a Friday earlier than yours, a different branch..." He broke off. "No. That is idle. We don't know enough."

"Say it anyway."

"The person who poured the coffee." He spoke carefully. "You assumed she was you."

"The handwriting was mine."

"The handwriting was yours," Jonas agreed, "and the mug was yours. And the note told you to trust the handwriting. People who forge things have a habit of overdoing the personal touch." He wiped his hands on a rag. "Do you know who in this station can copy your hand?"

"Nobody."

"Nobody who has tried, perhaps." He looked, then, at the cadmium glass, and the line in it. "I am going to lock the hold tonight, Ines. I am going to put the key in your pocket, not mine. And I am going to sleep in the mess where Dev can see me. That is a promise, and it is a promise with a witness."

She believed him. She went away and had lunch, a thing she had not done in three days, and sat in the sun on the lee side of the platform with her back against a warm pipe. At four in the afternoon she told Jonas, laughing a little, that she thought they had won.

She was wrong about almost everything, as it turned out.

At nine that night Pike called a meeting of the whole platform in the mess, nine people and a pot of tea, to announce that the supply boat would arrive on Monday with an inspection team from the mainland. He did not say why. He did not need to. Everyone in the room understood that when a director began his announcements with *regrettably*, the next word was *closure*. Jonas sat very still beside her. When the meeting broke up, he did not go to the mess bunk as he had promised. He stood in the corridor outside with his hand flat against the wall, and Ines, who had been watching him all evening, saw his face before he schooled it.

It was the face of a man who has run out of time.

"I'll sleep in the mess," Jonas said. "As I said."

"Good."

"Ines." He took her hand, turned it over, and held it for a moment between both of his, a thing he had never done in four years. "Whatever tomorrow looks like. You were right to come back."

She watched him walk away down the corridor, a tall tired man in a grey work jacket with a torn cuff, and she thought: *I have been told something and have not understood it.*

At midnight, in cabin thirty-one, she took the hold key from her pocket, where he had pressed it into her hand without a word during the meeting. It was cold and heavy and it was not the right key. It was the key to the machine shop.

She did not sleep. She put on Dev's navy coat and went down the outer stairs into the dark.
