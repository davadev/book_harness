# Chapter 3: What the Tide Kept

*Three days later*

The packet from the capital came in on the morning tide, three hours late and carrying one passenger who did not look like a man who had enjoyed the crossing.

Nora watched him from the end of the quay, where she had been standing since daybreak with her hands in her pockets and her collar turned up against the wind. The fog had gone in the night, leaving the harbour scrubbed and bright, and every rope and rail and gull stood out with the hard clarity of a thing seen after a long illness. The passenger came down the gangway with a leather bag in one hand and a folded umbrella in the other, though it had not rained in a week, and stood for a moment on the wet stones looking up at the headland and the white tower of Greywater Light. He was a lean man in his early thirties, with a thin face, a stubble of beard, and green eyes that she had last seen across a kitchen table when she was twenty-three.

"You look terrible," she said.

"You look exactly the same," said her brother. "It is the most depressing thing about you."

Tomas Voss set down his bag, and for a moment neither of them moved. Then he held out his arms and she walked into them, and they stood on the quay in the bright cold morning like two people who had agreed not to say anything about it. He smelled of train smoke and other people's tobacco. He had grown thinner. She could feel his shoulder blades through his coat.

"The Captain wrote to me," Tomas said into her hair. "He did not say much. He said that the chest had been found, and that I should come, and that if I did not come he would never forgive himself."

"He is a dramatic old man."

"He is a frightened one." Tomas stepped back and held her at arm's length, and she saw him taking her in, her face, her pocket, the way she stood. "You have it, don't you. The Key."

"How do you know?"

"You are standing the way Papa used to stand." He smiled, without much humour. "Like a person carrying a stone in each pocket and trying not to let anyone see."

---

They ate breakfast in the kitchen of the lighthouse, because it was warm and because it was the only place in Halvern where neither of them would be interrupted. Nora had laid the ledger on the table between the bread and the teapot. Tomas had read the first three entries and then, wordlessly, handed it back and gone to stand at the window, where he remained for a long time looking at the sea.

"There is more," Nora said. "In the back. Entries from the last year, in a different ink. Entries he wrote after the night he sailed."

"That is not possible."

"It is the same hand. It is dated eleven months after he vanished." She turned to the page and read, because she had read it enough times now to know it by heart. "'If you are reading this, the chest has been found, and I was right about the Captain, who is a better man than I deserved for a friend. Do not go through the door. I repeat, Nell, do not go through the door until you have spoken to the one person who has already been through it. She will tell you what it costs. The party in the capital is not what it seems. They did not write to me. I wrote to them, and I gave them the wrong date.'"

Tomas turned from the window. "Nell," he said.

"He always called me that."

"I know he did. I am only saying he did not call you Nora. Not once in the whole of your life, not in front of me." His voice was careful. "In the log, ten years ago, he did the same. He wrote my name, and the Captain's, and Ilse's. He did not write yours. Nell was never in that room, was she?"

She had been waiting for this. For three days, through the whole slow circling of her own thoughts, she had known that her brother would eventually ask it, and she had not decided how to answer.

"I was in the kitchen," she said. "I had a candle and a book. I was reading *Persuasion* for the fifth time."

"You were sixteen. You were two years older than me."

"You were fourteen."

"I am thirty," said Tomas Voss quietly, "and I am your elder brother, Nora. I was fourteen when you were six. I remember the harvest festival you keep talking about, the one where the Captain carried you on his shoulders. I remember because I was the one who had to carry the lantern."

The kitchen was very quiet. Outside a gull began to complain about something. Nora heard her own breath, shallow and fast, and made herself take another, slower one.

"That is not how I remember it," she said.

"No," Tomas said. "I do not suppose it is."

She looked at him, and then at the ledger, and then, because she could not help it, at her own hands, which lay on the table in the weak October sunlight like the hands of someone she had never properly met.

---

In the afternoon they went to see Ilse Kovar, who was waiting for them in the harbour master's office with the stove roaring and a fresh pot of her appalling coffee. She looked at Tomas for a long moment without speaking, and then she did something Nora had not seen her do in all the years of their acquaintance. She crossed the room and took his face in both her big raw hands and kissed him on the forehead.

"*Děkuju*," she said roughly. "For coming. I did not think you would."

"The Captain's letter was very persuasive."

"The Captain is a coward." She let him go. "He is also the only one of us who has been honest, and he did it by sending you a letter instead of coming to your door. I have told him so. Sit down. Both of you. We have until the first of November, and we are going to spend the afternoon being practical."

She had laid it out on the chart table: a map of the harbour, a copy of the letter from the capital, the ledger, the compass, and a stack of old harbour-board minutes that she had pillaged from the archive. Pip was posted at the window as lookout, with strict instructions to say nothing and see everything. The apprentice took this duty with a gravity that would have made Nora smile on any other day.

"This is what we know," said Ilse, tapping the map with a pencil. "The firm in the capital is called Haas and Wendel. They are what is called a depositary. They hold things for people, papers, jewellery, sealed boxes. Your father deposited the Key with them in the spring, ten years ago, the year in question. At least that is the date on their receipt. But the Key was on his belt the day he sailed, three years ago, and it was in his chest when it was found. So either the receipt is wrong, or the Key has been in two places at the same time."

"Or there are two keys," said Tomas.

"Or there are two keys." Ilse looked at him with approval. "The surveyor thinks like a surveyor. Good. Then there is the matter of the party who has claimed it. Haas and Wendel will not say who. But I wrote to a friend in the capital, a clerk at the registry of deeds, and she was able to tell me this much. The claim was lodged by a woman. Her name is on the document. Her name is Eleonore Voss."

For a moment Nora heard nothing at all.

"That is not possible," she said. "That is my mother's name."

"Your mother died of a fever when you were twelve."

"I know when she died."

"I am not suggesting otherwise." Ilse's voice was very gentle, which was worse than if she had shouted. "I am telling you what is written. A woman named Eleonore Voss has filed a claim on the Meridian Key, and she says that she is its rightful owner, and she says she has the original deposit receipt. The firm believes her. It is not for me to say what that means. I only know that your father wrote 'speak to the one person who has already been through it.' And there is exactly one person who fits that description in the whole of this town."

"Who?" said Tomas.

Ilse Kovar looked at Nora, and Nora, for the first time in her life, understood what it was to be afraid of her own name.

"Not who," said Ilse. "You asked who is in the story. I am asking whom the story is about. Nora. Where were you the night the lamp went out?"

---

They went down together at dusk, the three of them and the apprentice, who had refused to be left on the quay and who walked a little in front with the lantern, small and fierce and absurdly proud. Captain Brandt was waiting at the head of the stair. He had shaved, and put on his good coat, and the effect was that of an old ship that has been scraped and painted for its last voyage.

"I would like to come," he said. "If you will have me."

Nora regarded him for a long time. Then, because she was tired and because there was no one else, she took his arm.

"You carried me on your shoulders," she said. "At the festival. Tomas says it was him."

"I carried the both of you," said the Captain. "One on each side. You were arguing, as usual, about who was heavier."

"Then why does he remember it differently?"

"Because he is remembering the lantern," said Brandt, "and you are remembering the shoulders. It is the same night. You were both right. That is the trouble with memory, my dear. It is a good servant and a dreadful witness."

The Undercroft was colder than she remembered, and drier. The salt had been scraped from the walls since her last visit, leaving pale scars on the stone, and the rings had been stripped from the vault and carted away. Even the sound had changed. Where three days ago the whole passage had dripped and murmured, now it was silent, and their footsteps rang on the flags like footsteps in an empty church. At the junction where she had found the Captain with the chest, the passage bent right, and then right again, and ended at the door.

She did not need to look at the compass this time. She knew what it would do. She took it from her pocket and held it a moment in her palm, warm and heavy, and thought about the woman in the capital who had her mother's name, and about the girl in the nightdress who was not on the log, and about a father who had called her Nell and had never once, in a lifetime, told her the truth.

Then she set the Key into the hollow of the door, and turned it.

The door opened inward, with a sound like a long sigh. Beyond it there was no green light, no city, no water. There was a small, bare, round room, lit by a single oil lamp on a table. At the table, with her back to them, sat a woman writing in a book.

She was thin, and grey-haired, and she wore a dress that had been out of fashion for twenty years. When she heard them she did not turn at once. She finished her sentence, blotted it, and laid down her pen with the unhurried care of someone who has been expecting visitors for a very long time.

"There you are, Nell," said Eleonore Voss. "I wondered how long you would take."

And Nora Voss, who was twenty-six, and had green eyes, and had always been sure of her own history, looked into the face of the woman who was her mother, and saw her own face looking back, older by thirty years, and exactly the same.
