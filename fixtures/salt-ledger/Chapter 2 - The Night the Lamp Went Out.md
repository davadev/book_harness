# Chapter 2: The Night the Lamp Went Out

*Ten years earlier*

Tomas Voss was fourteen years old, and he had been told twice to go to bed.

He had not gone. Instead he sat on the third stair from the top of [[Greywater Light]], with his knees drawn up and his chin on them, watching a line of yellow light under the door of the lantern room. Behind that door his father was talking in the low flat voice he used when he was frightened and did not want anyone to know it. Tomas could not hear the words. He could only hear the shape of them, the way you can hear the shape of a sermon through a church wall.

His sister had gone to bed. Nora was two years older, sixteen and very sure of everything, and she had said that anyone who sat up on the stairs in the dark was a baby, and then she had gone down to the kitchen and sat up in the dark herself with a candle and a book, pretending to read. He knew this because he had seen the glow under her door when he came past. They were, he sometimes thought, the two most stubborn people in the whole of Halvern, and the only reason they had never had a proper quarrel was that neither would ever admit to being the one who had started it.

It was the last night of October. The harvest festival was over, the herring fleet was in, and the sea had that heavy, oily calm that old fishermen said was worse than any gale. The air in the tower was warm. That was the first wrong thing. In all his life Tomas could not remember the stone stairwell being warm in October, but tonight it breathed against his face like the inside of an animal, and the walls were faintly damp, and when he put his palm flat against the stone it was not cold.

The light under the door went out.

It did not flicker, and it did not fade. It simply stopped, like a sentence cut off in the middle, and the whole tower fell into a darkness so complete that Tomas, who had grown up on the headland and thought he knew every kind of night, put his hand in front of his face and could not see it. Far above him, the great lens, which had turned without a pause for forty years, stopped turning. He felt the silence arrive. It was not an absence of sound; it was something heavier, a thing with weight, that pressed on his ears.

Then his father's voice, quite calm now: "Tomas. Come up, please. And bring the small lamp."

He came up.

---

The lantern room was full of a pale light that came from nowhere. That was the second wrong thing. The lamp was dead, the lens was dark, the windows showed only black water and blacker sky, and yet he could see his father standing at the chart table as clearly as in daylight, and beside him, to Tomas's astonishment, stood Captain Brandt, who had no business being there at all.

The Captain was not a Captain yet. He was a lean, red-haired mate of fifty-odd, with a limp that had not had time to settle into his walk, and he was gripping the edge of the table as if the floor might tilt. Beside him stood a young woman in a pilot's oilskins, tall and thin and soaked to the bone, her short hair plastered to her skull. It took Tomas a moment to recognise Ilse Kovar, who in those days was only a harbour pilot and not yet the harbour master, and who looked, just then, like a person who had run a long way in the wrong direction.

"Close the door behind you," said his father. "And do not touch anything."

Edmund Voss was a man of medium height, narrow in the shoulder, with a stoop that came from forty years of ducking under lintels designed for shorter men. He wore his spectacles pushed up into his hair and a green wool jersey that Tomas's mother had knitted before she died. In one hand he held a silver compass, and as Tomas watched, he laid it very gently on the chart table and took his hand away, as a man sets down something that might go off.

"The water is wrong tonight," Edmund said. He did not say it to anyone in particular. "It came in from the east, and it is warm. I have measured it three times. I measured it with the glass and with my hand and with my own two eyes, and it is warm, and it is coming in from the east, and there is no east in the Halvern tide."

"There is no east in any tide," said Ilse Kovar. Her teeth were chattering. "The bar faces north-west. Water can come from the north or from the sea. Not from the east. There is nothing in the east but land."

"There was land," said Edmund Voss, "yesterday."

Nobody said anything. Tomas became aware that the pale light had a direction after all, and that it was coming up through the floor, through the iron grating beneath the lens where the old cable went down into the rock. It was the colour of water seen from underneath, green and very clear, and it moved over the ceiling in slow ripples. In the ripples, for a moment, he thought he saw shapes: arches, a long vault, an iron door.

"Papa," he said. "What is it?"

His father turned. For a long moment he looked at his son with an expression that Tomas would remember for the rest of his life, though he would never be able to say exactly what it was: part love, part fear, and part something like apology.

"I think," said Edmund Voss, "it is a door that has been opened from the other side."

The log is wrong about one thing, and Tomas has known it for ten years. He has never corrected it, because he is not sure he is right.

He remembers that his sister was there.

He remembers it clearly: Nora in the doorway of the lantern room in her nightdress, with her hair down and the candle still in her hand, saying *What is happening* in the voice she used for the cows when they got into the vegetable patch. He remembers his father saying *Nell, go back to bed*, and her not going. He remembers the green light on her face, and her eyes in it, wide and very pale, and the way she took one step toward the grating as if something down there had called her by name.

But the log lists four witnesses, and Nora is not one of them. And Nora herself, when he asked her years later, said she had slept through the whole thing, and had learned about the lamp only in the morning, from the Captain's face. She said it flatly and she did not look at him while she said it. Tomas has come to believe that one of them is lying, and that it is not the one he would have expected.

It is possible, he thinks, that he invented her. It is the sort of thing a grieving brother might do. He has had ten years to practise remembering the night, and memory, he has learned, is not a record but a habit; it rehearses what it has decided is important, and it throws away the rest. If you tell a story often enough you stop being able to tell which parts you saw and which parts you added. So when he says that Nora was there, he says it with an asterisk. He has never said it to anyone but himself.

---

They went down into the Undercroft at midnight, the five of them: Edmund Voss and the mate Brandt, the pilot Kovar, Tomas, who had refused to be left behind and had won the argument by standing in the doorway and saying nothing, and, if he is to be believed, a girl in a nightdress who walked three paces behind the others with her candle held high.

The stair that led down from the quay was awash. The water that covered the steps was warm as a bath, and clear as glass, and it smelled not of the sea but of something older and fainter, like the inside of a shell. At the bottom of the stair the iron gate stood open and the passage beyond ran down into the dark, and the light that came up out of it was the same pale green they had seen in the tower.

"It is a lot of salt," Ilse Kovar said, running a finger along the wall. "It has crusted. Look. It has been underwater for a very long time."

"No," said Edmund. "Look again. It has been underwater for a very short time. This is what the wall looks like after one night of the sea. It is not crusted. It is *growing*."

He was right. Where she had touched it, the white film was already thickening, spreading out from her fingertip in fine branching lines like frost on a pane. Tomas watched it creep across the stone toward the rings, and the rings began, very faintly, to ring: a high clear note, the sound of a finger drawn around the rim of a glass.

They walked on. The passage bent, and bent again, and the light grew stronger, until they came to the iron door.

It was tall, and shut, and there was a hollow in the middle of it the size of a man's palm. Edmund Voss took the silver compass from his pocket, and held it up, and Tomas saw his father's hand shake.

"I have wondered for twenty years," Edmund said, "what this was for. My father wondered, and his father before him. It has come down to us in the family as a keepsake, a pretty thing, a thing that points the wrong way. They told me it was broken. It was not broken. It was waiting."

"Waiting for what?" said Brandt.

"For the water to be wrong."

He set the compass into the hollow. For a heartbeat nothing happened, and Tomas let out a breath he had not known he was holding. Then the needle swung, and the door breathed in, and the light on the other side poured out over all of them like warm milk, and a sound began that was not a sound: a vast slow note, felt in the bones, the voice of something enormous clearing its throat after a very long sleep.

And through the door, in the green light, Tomas saw a city.

He has told this part so often that he can no longer tell it any other way. It was a city of white stone, built on terraces down the side of a valley, and every street of it was full of water, and every house of it was lit from inside. Boats moved along the streets, long and dark and silent. People stood on the roofs. They were looking up, all of them, at something above the roofs, and it took Tomas a moment to understand that they were looking at the underside of the sea.

He took a step toward the door. His father's hand closed on his collar.

"Not you," said Edmund Voss. "Not yet. Not for a long time, I hope."

He did not say what he meant. The next thing Tomas knew, the door was shut and the light was gone and the water was around his knees, and the Captain, who was still a mate, was carrying him up the stair on his back with one arm and the girl in the nightdress, or the memory of her, was gone as if she had never been.

---

They did not speak of it afterwards. That was the third wrong thing, and in some ways the worst. Edmund Voss relit the lamp the next morning and it burned perfectly. The water went down. The salt on the Undercroft walls was scraped away by the harbour board and carted off, and the whole matter was written up in the log in four lines and then, by common consent, left alone.

Brandt was promoted to Captain the following spring. Ilse Kovar became harbour master within the year. Edmund Voss went on keeping the light, and taught his children to count the oil in drops, and told them nothing more. Only once, on the anniversary, did Tomas catch him standing in the lantern room with the compass in his hand, turning it over and over, not looking at it.

"Papa," he said. "What did we see?"

"A tide," said his father, after a long time. "Only a tide, Tomas. A very old one. And it will come in again, one day, and somebody will have to decide whether to let it."

Tomas left Halvern at nineteen. He told people it was for work, and in a sense it was: he took a post as a surveyor with a firm in the capital, and for several years he drew maps of other people's coastlines with great accuracy and no pleasure. But he knew why he went. He went because he could not stand to live in a town where a door under the harbour might open at any moment, and where the only three people who knew about it had agreed never to speak.

He sent his sister a letter every month for seven years. She wrote back to every one. In none of the letters, on either side, did anyone mention the lamp.

Then, three years ago, their father went out in the herring boat at dawn with the Meridian Key on his belt, and the sea took him, and the Key with him, and Tomas stopped writing.
