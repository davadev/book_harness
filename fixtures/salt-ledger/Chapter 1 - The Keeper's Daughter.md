# Chapter 1: The Keeper's Daughter

The fog came into Halvern harbour the way it always did in October, not as a wall but as a slow forgetting. First the far end of the breakwater went, then the masts of the herring boats, then the clock tower on the customs house, until the whole town seemed to be dissolving into the grey water it had been built to hold back. From the lantern room of Greywater Light, Nora Voss watched it happen with the patience of someone who had watched it happen every autumn of her twenty-six years.

She had been up since four. The lamp did not need her at that hour, for the clockwork turned the great lens without help, but the clockwork needed oil, and oil was a thing her father had trusted nobody else to measure. Now that he was gone, it fell to her. She wiped her hands on a rag that smelled of whale-fat and brass polish and counted the drops as they fell into the reservoir: one, two, three, four, and then a pause, because the fifth always hung on the lip of the spout for longer than it should.

"You count them out loud, you know," said a voice from the stair. "Your father did the same. I used to think it was a prayer."

Nora did not turn round. She knew the voice, and she knew the tread that went with it, heavy on the left foot where an old injury had never quite healed. "It is a prayer, Captain. It is just a very dull one."

Captain Aldous Brandt came up through the hatch with the care of a man who no longer trusted ladders, and stood with one hand on the rail while he got his breath. He was a broad, weathered person of sixty or so, with white whiskers that stuck out like frost on a window and a coat the colour of wet slate. Under his arm he carried a flat parcel wrapped in oilcloth and tied with the kind of knots that sailors tie when they want a thing to stay tied.

"Ilse Kovar sent me," he said. "The harbour master. She would have come herself but she has a ship stuck on the bar and a lot of shouting to do."

"She could have sent a boy."

"She could. She sent me instead, which ought to tell you something." He held out the parcel. "This was found in the lining of your father's sea chest. The chest came up from the Undercroft yesterday, when they cleared the old customs vaults before the water got in. Nobody opened it until this morning."

Nora took the parcel. It was heavier than it looked, and cold, as if it had been lying against a stone floor for years. She laid it on the chart table beside the lamp-log and undid the knots one at a time, because her father had taught her never to cut a knot that somebody else had tied. The oilcloth fell open.

Inside was a book, small and thick, bound in cracked green leather that had gone black along the spine. Stamped into the cover in letters that had once been gilt were three words: THE SALT LEDGER. Beside the book, wrapped in its own square of flannel, lay a brass compass the size of a man's palm.

She knew the compass at once. Everybody in Halvern knew the compass. It was the Meridian Key, and it had hung from her father's belt on every day of her childhood, swinging against his hip as he climbed the tower stairs. When he vanished off the North Skerry three years ago, it had vanished with him. The harbour board had spent a whole winter arguing about whether to dredge for it.

"It was in the chest," Nora said slowly. "It was in the chest the whole time."

"So it appears."

"But he was wearing it. I saw him put it on, the morning he sailed. I stood on the quay and watched him fasten it to his belt."

Captain Brandt said nothing. He looked at the lens turning above them, at the long beams of light that swept out into the fog and found nothing to land on, and she understood that he was giving her time to think the same thought he had already thought. If the compass had been in the chest, then her father had not been wearing it. And if he had not been wearing it, then somebody had put it into that chest after he left, and had known where to hide it.

She picked up the ledger instead, because it was easier. The pages were thick and slightly greasy, ruled in faded blue, and the writing that covered them was her father's: small, upright, relentlessly neat. She turned to the first entry and read it aloud, her voice flat in the cold air of the lantern room.

"Tide at Halvern bar, high water, four hours after the moon crosses the meridian. Height, nineteen spans. Wind from the northwest, light. Salt in the lower reaches of the river for three miles above the ford." She stopped. "It is only a tide table."

"Is it?" said the Captain. "Read the next one."

The next entry was in the same hand, but the date beside it was ten years old, and the tide it recorded was not a tide at all. Nora read it twice before she trusted herself to speak.

"The water is wrong tonight," she said. "That is what he writes. 'The water is wrong tonight. It came in from the east and it was warm. The lamp went out for the first time in forty years and no one else saw it.' " She looked up. "The lamp has never gone out. Not once, not in all the years I have been here. My father would have told me."

"Would he?"

It was a fair question, and she disliked it for being fair. Her father had been a quiet man with a loud conscience, and over the long evenings of her girlhood he had told her a great many things, about the lamp, about the stars, about how to read the colour of a wave. But he had also been a man who kept his own counsel, and there had been doors in him that she had learned, early, not to try.

Outside, the foghorn on the breakwater began its long complaint. Down in the harbour a bell answered, then another. Halvern was waking up.

---

By eight o'clock the fog had settled for the day and the town went about its business inside it, the way people go about their business inside a bad mood. Nora walked down from the headland by the cliff path with the ledger in her satchel and the compass in her coat pocket, where she could feel its weight against her hip. Captain Brandt had gone ahead to speak with the harbour master. He had offered to wait for her, and she had refused, because she wanted to be alone for the length of the walk.

The path was slick with condensation and she took it carefully, as she had taken it a thousand times. On her left the headland fell away to nothing; on her right the gorse crouched against the rock, black and wet. Below her, somewhere in the grey, the sea was going about its old argument with the shore. She could hear it more than she could see it, a long sigh and a hiss, a long sigh and a hiss.

She thought about the word wrong. Her father had used it in a way that she had never heard him use it about the sea. The sea, to him, could be dangerous or generous or cruel, but it could not be wrong, any more than a mountain could be wrong. Wrong was a word for things that had gone against their own nature. He had written that the water was wrong, and then he had gone on keeping the light for another seven years as though nothing had happened.

At the bottom of the path she crossed the cobbled apron in front of the fish market, where the stalls were already steaming with the morning's catch, and a girl of about fourteen stepped out of the crowd and fell into step beside her. It was the apprentice from the harbour master's office, the one everybody called Pip, with her hair cropped short for the lice and her ink-stained fingers jammed into the pockets of a coat three sizes too big.

"Miss Voss," said Pip. "She said I was to fetch you. The harbour master. She says it's urgent, and she says to say it's not the ship."

"What is it, then?"

"She didn't say." Pip glanced sideways at Nora's pocket, and then quickly away again. "She just said you'd want to see it before the Captain did."

Nora stopped walking. "Before the Captain did? He is with her now."

"He was," said Pip. "He left. Went off toward the Undercroft with two men from the board. Looked like he was in a hurry." She hesitated. "He had the sea chest with him. The big one. They put it on a cart."

The cold in the compass had seemed to move up Nora's arm and settle somewhere in her chest. She had been given the ledger and the compass an hour ago by a man who had then, apparently, gone to remove the only other thing that might have explained them. She did not think the Captain was a villain. She had known him all her life, and he had carried her on his shoulders at the harvest festival when she was six. But she had also noticed, in the lantern room, that when she had asked where the chest had come from he had said "the Undercroft" with a slight pause, the way a man pauses before stepping onto ice.

"Take me to the harbour master," she said.

The harbour master's office stood at the seaward end of the long quay, a squat stone building with a slate roof and a single high window through which Ilse Kovar had, for twenty years, shouted at everything that moved in Halvern harbour. Inside it smelled of ink, wet wool, and the sour coffee that she brewed in a tin pot on the stove and drank by the gallon. Ilse herself was standing at the chart table with her sleeves rolled to the elbow and a pencil behind each ear, a tall, raw-boned woman with grey hair cut short and a face that looked as if it had been carved with a blunt chisel and then left out in the rain.

"There you are," she said. "Shut the door. Pip, go and see if the bar has moved. Go on."

Pip went. Ilse waited until the footsteps had faded along the quay, and then she took a folded paper from under a paperweight shaped like a lobster and pushed it across the table.

"This came in last night on the evening packet," she said. "Addressed to your father. It was held at the post office because nobody knew where to send it. I opened it. I will apologise for that when I have the time."

The paper was thin and expensive, and the handwriting on the envelope was the kind of looping copperplate that people learn in schools Nora had never attended. Inside was a single sheet, dated three days earlier and headed with the name of a firm in the capital.

*Dear Mr Voss, We regret to inform you that the instrument deposited with this house in the spring of the year in question has now been requested by a party whose claim we are not in a position to dispute. We would be obliged if you would present yourself at our offices, with the key, no later than the first of November. Failing this we shall be obliged to release the instrument as directed. We remain, etc.*

Nora read it twice. Then she laid it on the table and put both her palms flat on either side of it, the way her father used to do when a piece of news was too big for him to look at straight.

"The instrument," she said. "The key. They mean the compass."

"I thought so."

"But my father has been missing for three years. And this says 'the spring of the year in question.' What year? In question for whom?" She looked up. "Ilse, who is the party?"

"I do not know. That is why I wanted you to see it before anyone else did." Ilse picked up one of the pencils from behind her ear and began to turn it over in her fingers, a thing she did when she was deciding how much to say. "I will tell you what I know, and then I will tell you what I think, and you can decide for yourself which is which."

"Go on."

"What I know is this. The Captain was in here at half past six this morning, before the fog had properly come in, asking whether anyone had delivered a letter for your father. Before the packet was even sorted. I told him no. I lied. I do not entirely know why I lied, except that he asked the question like a man who already knew the answer."

"And what do you think?"

"I think," said Ilse, "that your father put that compass into his own chest on purpose, and then walked out of Halvern without it, and that the Captain has known about it for three years." She set the pencil down. "I think the Captain and your father were in something together, and I do not think it was anything as simple as smuggling."

Nora found that she was holding the edge of the chart table so hard that her knuckles ached. She made herself let go. "He carried me on his shoulders," she said, and heard how foolish it sounded.

"I know," said Ilse. "He carried half the children in this town. It is a thing he does. That is exactly what worries me about him."

---

She did not go back to the lighthouse. Instead she walked the length of the quay in the fog, past the herring boats with their nets hung out like grey laundry, past the chandler's and the rope walk and the long low shed where the fishwives gutted the catch, until she came to the stone steps that led down into the Undercroft.

The Undercroft was older than the town. Nobody quite agreed who had built it. The harbour board said it had been a medieval customs vault; the old women who sold eels at the market said it had been a church, drowned when the river changed course; and her father, in one of his rare unguarded moods, had once told her it was neither, and that it was something much older than either, and that the only honest word for it was a machine. She had laughed. He had not.

The iron gate at the foot of the steps stood open. Beyond it the vaulted passage ran away into darkness, and from somewhere deep inside came the sound of men's voices and the scrape of something heavy being dragged over stone. Nora lit the small lantern that she kept in her satchel for lamp-room emergencies, held it up, and went in.

The air changed as she went down. It grew colder, and then, oddly, warmer, and the walls began to sweat a pale mineral film that glittered in the lantern light like frost on a window. Salt, she thought, and tasted it on her lips. Every surface was white with it. The ceiling, the floor, the vaults on either side, the iron rings set into the stone at intervals as though for mooring boats. A long time ago, she thought, this place had been underwater, and it had not been underwater for the usual reasons.

"Miss Voss." The voice came out of the dark ahead of her, quiet and almost gentle. "I did hope you would not follow me."

Captain Brandt was standing at the junction of two passages, with the sea chest at his feet and a lantern of his own set down on the lid. The two men from the harbour board stood a little behind him, their faces closed. The Captain had taken his hat off, and without it he looked older and somehow smaller.

"You gave me the compass," Nora said. "You gave me the ledger. And then you came down here to take the chest away. Which was it, Captain? Did you want me to have them, or did you not?"

"Both," said Aldous Brandt. "God help me, both."

He sat down heavily on the chest, like a man whose legs had made the decision for him, and for a moment nobody spoke. Somewhere in the walls water was dripping, slowly, a drop and a pause and then another drop.

"Your father asked me to keep him out of sight for as long as I could bear it," the Captain said at last. "Then he asked me to give you the ledger when the time came. And he said the time would come when the chest was found. That is all he said. I have been waiting three years for somebody to find the chest. I did not know I would be so afraid of it when they did."

"Afraid of what?"

"Of what the ledger says." He raised his eyes to hers, and they were pale, and wet, and afraid. "I never read it, Nora. I swear that on the light. He told me that if I read it I would never be able to put it down, and I have never doubted him. But I know what's in it, because I know what he was looking for. He was trying to understand why the water was wrong."

The drip in the wall went on. Nora waited for the next drop and it did not come, and she realised that the whole passage had gone still, as if the stone itself were listening.

"Tell me," she said.

"It is easier to show you." The Captain stood, and one of the harbour men made a small protesting sound, and he waved him quiet. He lifted his lantern and walked on down the passage, toward the place where it bent away to the right, and Nora followed. They went past the rings and the white walls and the places where something had been scraped away from the stone long ago, and after what seemed a long while they came to a door.

It was an iron door, taller than a man and set flush into the vault, and it was shut. There was no handle. In the centre of it, at about the height of her heart, was a round hollow the size of a man's palm, lined with a thin film of green verdigris, and it was exactly the size of the compass in her pocket.

Nora did not remember taking the compass out. She was simply standing there with it in her hand, and the brass was warm now, warmer than her own skin, and the needle inside the glass had stopped trembling and pointed steadily at the door.

"He told me," the Captain whispered, "that it was not a compass at all."

"What is it, then?"

"A key," said Aldous Brandt. "The Meridian Key. And it has been waiting for you."

Behind them, far back along the passage, a single drop of water fell into the dark, and was answered by another, and another, and then by the long slow sound of the tide beginning, impossibly, to come in.
